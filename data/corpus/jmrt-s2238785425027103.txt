A B S T R A C T
This study examines the combined influence of dust composition, humidity, and air quality on the performance 
of photovoltaic (PV) systems in Jubail, Saudi Arabia—an archetypal arid coastal region. Four dust types 
(montmorillonite, kaolinite, bentonite, and natural dust) were systematically deposited on polycrystalline PV 
modules at varying surface densities (1.0–7.0 g/m2) under controlled outdoor conditions in September 2025. 
Concurrently, atmospheric parameters such as relative humidity, air quality index (AQI), and surface tempera­
ture were continuously monitored. SEM-EDX analysis revealed distinct mineralogical compositions: natural dust 
was rich in silica (25.37 %) and calcium oxide (30.52 %), whereas montmorillonite contained high iron content 
(62.67 %), influencing both optical transmittance and heat accumulation. Experimental results showed that 
natural dust caused the greatest power loss (48 % at 6 g/m2), while montmorillonite produced the highest 
thermal stress (surface temperature reaching 40.4 ◦C). Elevated humidity (>60 %) further exacerbated efficiency 
losses (15–30 %) due to enhanced dust adhesion and moisture-induced conductive paths. A modified Kaldel­
lis–Kapsali exponential model (R2 > 0.95) accurately predicted the nonlinear efficiency decline relative to dust 
density and composition. Diurnal analysis indicated peak generation during low-humidity morning hours 
(8:00–12:00), with afternoon efficiency dropping by 20–25 % owing to increased moisture and AQI. Correlation 
analysis identified AQI (R = −0.83) as a stronger predictor of performance degradation than humidity (R =
−0.77). These findings establish critical maintenance thresholds (4.0 g/m2 dust density) and support the 
development of tailored mitigation strategies, including hydrophobic coatings and thermally resistant materials, 
for sustainable PV operation in harsh desert climates.
1. Introduction
Photovoltaic (PV) systems have emerged as a cornerstone of global 
renewable energy strategies, providing a sustainable alternative to fossil 
fuels by directly converting sunlight into electricity [1,2]. Their adop­
tion has surged in regions with high solar irradiance, such as arid and 
semi-arid areas, where sunlight is abundant and consistent. However, 
these regions also present unique environmental challenges that 
significantly impair PV performance. Among these challenges, dust 
accumulation, humidity, and fluctuating air quality stand out as critical 
factors that degrade efficiency and reduce longevity. The potential of 
solar energy is enormous, with the Earth receiving approximately 3,400, 
000 EJ of solar energy annually. The total solar flux reaching the Earth’s 
surface amounts to 1.08 × 108 W, while the solar constant is measured 
at 1367 W/m2 (see Schemes 1 and 2).
While extensive research has explored individual stressors, the syn­
ergistic effects of these factors, particularly in coastal arid environments 
like Jubail, Saudi Arabia, remain understudied. This introduction re­
views past literature on PV performance degradation, identifies gaps in 
understanding, and outlines the objectives of the current study to 
address these lacunae. The transition to solar energy is driven by its 
potential to mitigate climate change and meet increasing energy

Between 2005 and 2018 alone, global greenhouse gas emissions 
increased by 23 %, leading to environmental problems such as rising 
temperatures on the Earth’s surface. This is one of the reasons why many 
countries are turning to renewable energy sources that emit no emis­
sions after they are installed [3].
By 2022, global solar capacity exceeded 1 TW, with desert regions in 
the Middle East, North Africa, and Asia leading large-scale installations 
due to their high solar potential [4]. However, arid and coastal envi­
ronments, while rich in sunlight, expose PV systems to harsh conditions 
[5–7]. Dust storms, high humidity, and temperature extremes are 
frequent in these regions, creating a paradox: optimal solar resources 
coexist with environmental stressors that drastically reduce energy 
output. For instance, in Saudi Arabia, dust deposition can reduce PV 
efficiency by 20–40 % within weeks [8–10], while humidity levels 
exceeding 60 % exacerbate dust adhesion, compounding losses [11,12]. 
Saudi Arabia, with its abundant solar resources, represents a prime 
example of this solar energy potential. The Kingdom receives some of 
the world’s highest levels of solar irradiation, with annual direct normal 
irradiance (DNI) values ranging from 1900 to 2500 kWh/m2/year across 
different regions. The country’s Vision 2030 program has set ambitious 
renewable energy targets, aiming to generate 50 % of its electricity from 
renewable sources by 2030, with solar energy playing a pivotal role in 
this transformation.
Solar energy plays a key role in providing sustainable and renewable 
energy solutions, reducing dependence on fossil fuels and contributing 
to environmental protection [13].
Early studies by El-Shobokshy and Hussein [14] demonstrated that 
dust composition—such as silica, calcite, or organic matter—affects 
light transmittance differently. For example, calcium carbonate (CaCO3) 
reflects ultraviolet light, while iron oxide (Fe2O3) absorbs infrared ra­
diation, increasing panel temperature. These findings were corroborated 
by Sarver et al. [15], who classified dust into “reflective” (e.g., silica) 
and “absorptive” (e.g., iron-rich) types, each with distinct degradation 
mechanisms. In desert regions, natural dust often contains high pro­
portions of silica (SiO2) and calcium oxide (CaO), as shown in studies 
from the Arabian Peninsula [16]. These minerals form cohesive layers in 
the presence of humidity, creating a cement-like film that is difficult to 
remove. For example, Al-Hasan et al. [16] reported that calcium-rich 
dust reduced PV efficiency by 35 % in Kuwait due to its hygroscopic 
nature, which enhances adhesion. Similarly, work in Arizona by Sayigh 
et al. [17] found that silica-dominated dust caused a 50 % decline in 
output over a six-week period. These studies highlight the importance of 
analyzing dust mineralogy when developing mitigation strategies. Hu­
midity exacerbates dust-related degradation by acting as a binding 
agent. When relative humidity exceeds 60 %, capillary forces between 
dust particles and the PV surface strengthen, creating durable layers that 
resist wind or manual cleaning [18]. This phenomenon was quantified 
by Maghami et al. [19], who observed that humidity levels above 70 % 
in Malaysia increased dust adhesion by 30 %, resulting in a 25 % drop in 
power output. The interplay between humidity and dust composition 
further complicates this issue. For instance, Bentonite clay—a common 
dust component in arid regions—swells under moisture, increasing its 
light-blocking capacity [20]. Moreover, adsorbed water molecules on 
PV surfaces create parasitic electrical pathways, reducing charge carrier 
mobility and increasing recombination rates [21]. A study in coastal 
India by Rao et al. [22] demonstrated that humidity-induced surface 
moisture reduced open-circuit voltage (Voc) by 12 %, independent of 
dust accumulation. These findings highlight the dual role of humidity: it 
enhances dust adhesion and directly impairs electrical performance.
Air quality, often measured by the Air Quality Index (AQI), in­
fluences PV efficiency through light scattering and soiling. High con­
centrations of PM2.5 and PM10 particles—common in regions with 
industrial activity or frequent dust storms—attenuate incoming solar 
irradiance before it reaches the panel surface. Anna et al. [23] modeled 
this effect in Beijing, showing that PM2.5 levels above 150 μg/m3 
reduced irradiance by 18 %, correlating with a 15 % decline in PV 
output. Similarly, in Cairo, Elminir et al. [24] found that airborne 
particulates from urban pollution compounded dust-related losses, 
resulting in a 22 % reduction in annual energy yield. The combined 
impact of dust deposition and airborne particulates creates a “double 
penalty” for PV systems. For example, in a study combining experi­
mental and computational approaches, Darwish et al. [25] demon­
strated that soiling and aerosol scattering synergistically reduced 
efficiency by 35 % in Dubai. These results emphasize the importance of 
integrating air quality monitoring into PV performance assessments.
Arid and coastal environments, while rich in sunlight, expose PV 
systems to harsh conditions. Dust storms, high humidity, and tempera­
ture extremes are frequent in these regions, creating a paradox: optimal 
solar resources coexist with environmental stressors that drastically 
reduce energy output [26–28]. For instance, while Javed et al. [20] 
explored humidity’s role in dust adhesion, they did not consider how

2. Experimental
2.1. Materials
Four distinct dust samples were used to evaluate their impact on PV 
performance: montmorillonite, kaolinite, bentonite, and natural dust. 
The montmorillonite, kaolinite, and bentonite clays were sourced from 
Bata India Limited, Patnagar, India, as commercially available mineral 
powders in industrial and academic research. Each was certified for ≥99 
% purity and sieved to a particle size of ≤45 μm for uniform application. 
The natural dust sample was manually collected from glass surfaces 
exposed to ambient conditions in Jubail, Saudi Arabia, to represent 
realistic particulate fallout in an arid urban environment. Dust mass per 
unit area was precisely controlled using an analytical digital balance 
with a sensitivity of ±0.001 g. Sterilized quartz glass slides (dimensions: 
75 × 25 × 1 mm) were used as substrates for thermal measurements. The 
uniformity and granulometry of all dusts were confirmed using SEM 
imaging, ensuring representative and reproducible surface coverage 
across all test stages.
2.2. Experimental setup and location
This study was conducted in Jubail, Saudi Arabia—an arid, high-dust 
coastal city—where environmental stressors such as windblown dust, 
high humidity, and temperature fluctuations frequently affect photo­
voltaic (PV) systems. The study was conducted at two primary locations, 
with one being a field testing site located in Jubail Industrial City, Saudi 
Arabia, at geographic coordinates 27◦00′N and 49◦40′E. This site is 
located at an elevation of 6 m above sea level and is classified as BWh 
(Hot Desert Climate) according to the K¨oppen climate classification. 
Characterized by its coastal urban setting, the site frequently experi­
ences dust events, which are significant for the environmental condi­
tions under investigation. A 20W polycrystalline PV panel was selected 
for outdoor performance testing (Table 1) and installed on a rooftop at a 
fixed 30◦tilt, facing south, to maximize solar irradiance exposure. The 
investigation was conducted over two distinct periods: September 
15–29, 2025, and September 9–24, 2025, to capture the variability in 
atmospheric conditions. The panel was exposed to open-sky conditions 
without shade obstructions to reflect real-world operating scenarios. 
This location allowed for natural dust accumulation and direct inter­
action with sunlight, ambient temperature, and humidity. The goal of 
the setup was to simulate operational PV systems commonly found in the 
region and evaluate how naturally occurring environmental conditions 
degrade efficiency over time, with a particular focus on the impact of air 
quality and particulate matter on energy production.

2.5. Temperature measurement protocol
Sterilized quartz glass slides were used as test substrates to evaluate 
the thermal effects induced by dust. Each slide was uniformly coated 
with 1.0 g/m2 of a specific dust type, then exposed to direct sunlight for 
1 h during midday. Temperature measurements were taken from the top 
(dust-exposed) and bottom (shaded) surfaces using a GM320 infrared 
thermometer to isolate thermal contributions from the dust. The slides 
were elevated slightly above ground level to prevent heat transfer from 
the ground, maintaining focus on solar-induced heating. This experi­
mental design minimized confounding variables and accurately 
measured the heat retention properties of each dust type. Temperature 
measurements were taken five times and then averaged. Data revealed 
how dust composition influenced panel surface temperatures, which are 
known to correlate inversely with solar efficiency. This protocol helped 
isolate the thermal mechanisms behind dust-induced performance losses 
in solar panels by identifying which dust types contribute to more sig­
nificant heating.
2.6. Dust characterization
The physical and chemical compositions of the dust samples were 
analyzed using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) coupled with En­
ergy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) via the JEOL JSM-6610 sys­
tem, operated at 30 kV under vacuum. Montmorillonite, kaolinite, 
bentonite, and natural dust samples were prepared and mounted on 
aluminum stubs with carbon adhesive. SEM imaging provided infor­
mation on surface morphology and particle size, while EDX analysis 
revealed the elemental composition. Results showed that natural dust 
was rich in silica (SiO2), calcium (CaO), and trace amounts of iron (Fe), 
indicating a high light-scattering potential. In contrast, montmorillonite 
exhibited elevated iron content (62.67 % Fe), suggesting enhanced 
thermal absorption. These compositional differences helped explain 
variations in the optical and thermal performance of each dust type 
when deposited on PV surfaces. Understanding these properties was 
essential for correlating physical characteristics of airborne particulates 
with their impact on solar panel efficiency.
2.7. Equipment specifications
Various precise instruments were used to measure electrical, ther­
mal, and environmental parameters. Electrical output was measured 
using a PeakTech P2015A digital multimeter, capable of measuring up 
to 1000 V AC and 10 A, with ±0.5–2.0 % accuracy. A GM320 infrared 
thermometer measured PV surface temperatures with ±1.5 ◦C accuracy 
and a range of −50 ◦C–380 ◦C. Solar irradiance was estimated using the 
PeakTech 5165 digital lux meter, which measures 0–200,000 lux with 
±3 % accuracy. Airborne particle concentration was measured using the 
LKC-1000S + laser particle counter, which is capable of detecting PM2.5 
and PM10 with a resolution of 0.1 μg/m3. The 20W BEST-20W poly­
crystalline solar panel had a rated open-circuit voltage of 21.1 V, short- 
circuit current of 1.29 A, and a tolerance of ±5 %. These high-accuracy 
tools ensured reliable data acquisition across all test conditions and 
environmental variables.

2.8. Environmental monitoring
Continuous environmental monitoring was essential for linking 
performance changes to ambient conditions. An LKC-1000S + air quality 
detector was employed to measure particulate matter concentrations 
(PM2.5 and PM10), which were used to calculate the AQI. The sensor 
was positioned near the PV array to capture representative local air 
quality data. Ambient temperature and humidity were logged using 
onboard meteorological sensors at each measurement interval, ensuring 
synchronized data for all performance metrics. This environmental data 
was then correlated with daily variations in voltage, current, and effi­
ciency. By analyzing trends across the two study periods, the research 
evaluated how atmospheric changes, such as dust storms or humidity 
spikes, directly influenced PV output. This approach provided a real- 
world perspective on how external stressors cumulatively impact solar 
panel performance, offering valuable insights for optimizing solar en­
ergy systems in environments characterized by high dust and fluctuating 
air quality.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Impact of dust deposition on power output and efficiency
The effect of different dust types on the power output of solar panels 
under outdoor conditions was investigated and illustrated in Fig. 1. The 
figure illustrates the inverse relationship between the surface density of 
dust and the power output of solar cells. As dust accumulation increased, 
power output consistently declined for all tested dust types—bentonite, 
kaolinite, montmorillonite, and natural dust. This decline is primarily 
due to dust obstructing incoming sunlight, which limits the photons 
from reaching the active surface of the photovoltaic cell and reduces the 
generation of electrical energy. Natural dust had the most pronounced 
impact among the four types, reducing output power from around 5 W to 
nearly 1 W at the highest dust concentration (approximately 6 g/m2). 
This suggests that natural dust, likely comprising a range of particle sizes 
and compositions, has a greater capacity to block solar radiation. 
Kaolinite and bentonite resulted in moderate power losses, while 
montmorillonite caused the least decline, preserving a relatively higher 
power output even as dust density increased. At 6 g/m2, kaolinite caused 
the highest power reduction (52.6 %), while natural dust reached 48 % 
at just 5 g/m2. However, natural dust showed the most rapid decline at 
lower densities (<4 g/m2), making it the dominant threat in real-world 
operation, where dust loads are typically moderate. These results un­
derscore that the type of dust and its characteristics, such as particle size, 
shape, and composition, significantly impact solar panel performance. 
Therefore, understanding regional dust composition is crucial in eval­
uating and maintaining photovoltaic system efficiency in arid and semi- 
arid environments.
3.2. Efficiency vs. dust density (exponential decline)
Fig. 2 illustrates the variation in solar panel efficiency as a function 
of increasing dust density for four different dust types: bentonite, 
kaolinite, montmorillonite, and natural dust. The study found an 
apparent degradation in solar panel efficiency with rising dust accu­
mulation, with the maximum efficiency of 11.57 % recorded under clean 
surface conditions. As dust density reached higher values (up to 6 g/m2), 
a gradual but noticeable decrease in efficiency was observed. Among the 
tested dust types, kaolinite resulted in the sharpest efficiency drop, 
reaching a minimum of 5.47 %, which represents a decline of approxi­
mately 6.1 percentage points. This suggests kaolinite’s fine particle size 
or high opacity significantly impedes sunlight transmission. Montmo­
rillonite demonstrated better performance stability, maintaining effi­
ciency above 10 % at a dust density of 1.3 g/m2 and only dropping by 
about 3.1 % at higher densities, indicating its relatively less obstructive 
nature. Bentonite and natural dust exhibited intermediate effects, with 
efficiencies decreasing to approximately 5.64 % and 6.1 %, respectively. 
These trends further emphasize the importance of dust characteristics in 
determining photovoltaic efficiency. The physical and chemical prop­
erties of dust, including particle size, morphology, mineral composition, 
and surface adhesion, influence light transmittance and absorption. The 
data also reinforce that dust accumulation remains a significant chal­
lenge for solar energy harvesting, particularly in regions susceptible to 
frequent dust exposure. Regular cleaning or anti-soiling technologies 
could be essential to maintain system performance in such areas.

Fig. 7a presents the relationship between photovoltaic (PV) power

71.1 % and an AQI of 115. Conversely, the lowest power (14.39 W) was 
recorded on 19 September, when both humidity and AQI were high (88 
% and 161, respectively). This inverse relationship indicates that 
elevated atmospheric water vapor and particulate concentration reduce 
the transmittance of solar radiation reaching the PV surface, as sup­

Interestingly, on 21 September, the power output rises to 17.09 W, 
despite a relatively high humidity (82.6 %) but a low AQI (58), sug­

induced attenuation. This confirms the finding by Elminir et al. [24] 
that particulate pollution exerts a more pronounced effect on solar 
irradiance loss than humidity alone. Finally, Fig. 7a demonstrates that 
PV power output is not solely humidity-dependent, but rather the result 
of interacting atmospheric factors, where both high RH and high AQI 
jointly suppress energy conversion through the absorption, scattering, 
and surface shading of light particles.

was moderately lower (163 vs. 197 earlier), again emphasizing the

3.8. Surface and rear temperature variations across dust types
Table 3 presents the front and rear surface temperatures of quartz 
substrates coated with different dust types. It is important to note that 
the measured temperature differences between samples are within the 
±1.5 ◦C accuracy range of the infrared thermometer. Therefore, while 
observed trends are discussed below, these temperature data alone 
cannot be presented as statistically significant proof of differential 
heating. This pattern is attributed to montmorillonite’s high iron content 
(62.67 % Fe, as confirmed by EDX analysis), which enables efficient 
solar absorption and high thermal conductivity, allowing heat to 
transfer through the substrate. In contrast, while exhibiting a similarly 
high front surface temperature (40.3 ◦C), kaolinite maintains a lower 
rear temperature (39.1 ◦C), indicating surface-level heat retention due 
to its aluminosilicate composition and relatively poor thermal conduc­
tivity. Bentonite, which contains moderate levels of sodium, magne­
sium, and calcium, exhibits balanced thermal behavior with front and 
rear temperatures of 39.9 ◦C and 39.2 ◦C, respectively. Natural dust, 
though compositionally heterogeneous, achieves moderate heating 
(front: 39.8 ◦C; rear: 39.7 ◦C), likely due to its dominant silica and cal­
cium oxide content, which reflects some radiation but retains heat 
within its particulate matrix. The clean substrate consistently records 
the lowest thermal profile, underscoring the thermal burden imposed by 
dust layers. These findings are crucial because elevated surface tem­
peratures degrade PV performance by increasing electron-hole recom­
bination rates, lowering open-circuit voltage, and accelerating material 
aging.
Fig. 8 presents SEM images and EDX spectra of four materials: (a) 
natural dust, (b) bentonite, (c) montmorillonite, and (d) kaolinite. The 
SEM images reveal distinct morphological features. Natural dust (a) 
exhibits irregular, heterogeneous particles, consistent with its environ­
mental origin. Bentonite (b) and montmorillonite (c), both smectite 
clays, display characteristic platy or layered structures with fine, 
aggregated particles, as indicated by the 1 μm scale bars. Kaolinite (d), a 
1:1 layered clay, exhibits larger, more defined hexagonal platelets, as 
noted in the 5 μm scale bar, suggesting coarser particle sizes or aggre­
gated stacks. The EDX spectra (Spectra 6–9) share a full-scale intensity of 
285 counts, implying comparable detection conditions. Typical clay 
mineral compositions are evident, with bentonite and montmorillonite 
spectra likely highlighting Al, Si, and O peaks, and possible traces of Mg 
or Fe in montmorillonite. Kaolinite is spectrum aligns with its simpler 
Al–Si–O structure. Depending on its source, natural dust may show 
additional elements (e.g., C, Ca, or Fe). The combined SEM-EDX data 
underscores the link between morphology (e.g., platy vs. irregular) and 
composition (elemental signatures), which is critical for differentiating 
these materials in environmental or industrial contexts. The results 
showed that covering surfaces with different types of dust resulted in 
higher temperatures than the clean film. This effect is attributed to the 
differences in the chemical and physical properties of each dust type, 
such as color, absorption, mineral content, and particle size. Soil surface 
temperature depends on the intensity of solar radiation, which causes 
temperature variations. Temperature changes depend on factors such as 
air temperature, humidity, albedo, soil color, and soil specific heat [53]. 
Soil has a moderate specific heat value, depending on its composition, 
which enables it to absorb and store thermal energy. Kaolinite and 
montmorillonite films were observed to have the highest temperatures, 
suggesting that clay-rich dust may have a greater capacity to absorb 
thermal energy from solar radiation than other types of dust. On the 
other hand, natural dust and bentonite showed relatively moderate 
temperature increases, suggesting that the diverse composition may 
reduce direct energy absorption compared to other minerals. The 
composition details of the dust were determined using energy-dispersive 
X-ray (EDX) analysis, and the results are presented in Table 4.
3.9. Comparative analysis of dust and humidity impacts on PV 
performance
The experimental results demonstrate that natural dust deposition 
causes a 48 % reduction in PV panel efficiency at the maximum tested 
concentration (6 g/m2). This finding aligns closely with recent work by 
Al-Sharafi et al. [9] who reported 45–50 % efficiency losses for 
silica-rich dust under similar deposition conditions in Riyadh. The 
comparable results between coastal and inland sites suggest that 
silica-dominated dust maintains its light-blocking properties across 
different arid environments, regardless of specific local conditions.
However, our observed 15–30 % efficiency reductions under high 
humidity conditions (>60 % RH) exceed the 10–20 % range reported by 
Javed et al. [20] for inland desert locations. This discrepancy likely 
stems from Jubail’s coastal environment, where the combination of 
marine aerosols and hygroscopic dust components (particularly calcium 
oxide) creates stronger moisture adsorption and cementation effects. 
The presence of chloride ions (5.16 wt% as determined by EDX analysis) 
in our natural dust samples further supports this coastal-specific 
behavior, as these salts are known to enhance water absorption.
Regarding thermal effects, the measured 1.0 ◦C temperature increase 
for montmorillonite-coated panels corroborates Kazem’s findings for 
iron-rich dusts. However, our values are notably lower than the 2–3 ◦C 
increases reported in desert interior studies [7]. We attribute this dif­
ference to Jubail’s coastal wind patterns (average 4.2 m/s sea breezes), 
which provide additional convective cooling absent in inland locations. 
This thermal moderation effect, while beneficial for PV efficiency, may 
be offset by the increased dust adhesion from salt-laden winds.
4. Conclusion
This comprehensive study elucidates the complex interplay between 
dust composition, humidity, and air quality in governing photovoltaic 
performance degradation within arid coastal environments, using 
Jubail, Saudi Arabia, as a representative case study. The experimental 
findings establish that environmental stressors operate synergistically 
rather than independently, with dust mineralogy serving as the primary 
determinant of both optical obstruction and thermal loading mecha­
nisms. Mineralogical analysis via SEM-EDX revealed distinct composi­
tional profiles that directly correlate with performance degradation 
patterns. Natural dust, characterized by high silica (25.37 %) and cal­
cium oxide (30.52 %) content, emerged as the most detrimental 
contaminant, inducing a 48 % power loss at a deposition density of 6 g/ 
m2 through combined light scattering and hygroscopic cementation. The 
calcium-rich fraction proved particularly problematic in coastal settings, 
where elevated ambient humidity (40–65 % RH) transforms loose

dominant influence on performance degradation, with AQI exhibiting a 
stronger negative correlation with efficiency (R ≈−0.86) than humidity 
alone (R ≈−0.78). At AQI levels exceeding 160, the combined effects of 
light scattering by airborne aerosols and surface soiling reduced con­
version efficiency below 10 %, even under moderate dust deposition 
densities (3–4 g/m2). This finding highlights a “double penalty” mech­
anism wherein atmospheric particulates attenuate incident irradiance 
before it reaches the panel surface, while simultaneously depositing as 
soiling layers that further obstruct transmission. The combined analysis 
indicates that while both humidity and air pollution negatively influ­
ence photovoltaic efficiency, the impact of AQI is more dominant 
because it operates through both atmospheric attenuation and physical 
soiling of the PV surface. These results reinforce global findings 
emphasizing that environmental control—through regular cleaning and 
monitoring of ambient air quality—can mitigate output losses and 
improve long-term system reliability in humid and polluted.
